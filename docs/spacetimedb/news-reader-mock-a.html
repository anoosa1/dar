News reader — A: three-pane list and reader

Direction A is a classic three-pane reader inside the selected frame (unified look B): sidebar with feeds and folders, a dense article list in the middle sheet, and the article in the right sheet, where every Dar app shows details. It favours scanning many feeds quickly, like Nextcloud News in compact mode. Both directions share the feature set below; they differ in how you read.

  • Feeds: RSS 0.9–2.0, Atom and JSON Feed, autodiscovery from a site address, folders, rename, move, pause and unsubscribe.
  • Reading: unread, starred and all views; mark read on open or on scroll, mark all read up to what is loaded, keep unread, undo.
  • Full text: per feed, fetch the article page and extract the readable text server-side; sanitized HTML, remote images proxied or blocked by choice.
  • Updates: background refresh every 15 min–24 h with conditional GET (ETag/Last-Modified), per-feed back-off, and visible failing-feed status with retry.
  • Media: podcast and video enclosures with an inline player that remembers position; images open in the shared viewer.
  • Retention: keep read articles for a chosen number of days or items per feed; starred items are always kept.
  • Portability: OPML import and export, starred export; a Nextcloud News API v1.3–compatible endpoint with app passwords so existing mobile clients can sync.
  • Everywhere: article titles in the shared cross-app search, share to Notes, keyboard shortcuts (j/k, s, m, o, Shift+A, r), phones and both themes.
  • Safety: fetches are server-side with private/loopback addresses refused, size and time limits, no cookies or credentials; per-account subscriptions with revision checks.

The classic three-pane reader. Feeds and folders sit in the sidebar with unread counts; the middle sheet is a dense list grouped by day; the selected article opens in the right sheet like any other Dar detail. Opening an article marks it read; j/k move through the list and the reader follows.

The state of Linux namespaces in 2026

By Jonathan Corbet·Today 9:12·11 min read

Namespaces were added to the kernel one at a time over more than a decade, and the result shows: each has its own rules for creation, ownership and teardown. This year's work has mostly been about making them compose.

The most visible change is that idmapped mounts are now supported by every major filesystem, which removes the last reason many container runtimes had for copying or chowning image layers.
